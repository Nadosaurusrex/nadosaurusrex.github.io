subtitle: "We now have the tools. The question is who gets handed them."

In January I wrote that talent isn’t rare and access is. I still believe it, but I’ve started to think the sentence was missing a piece. The tool has been invented, and that part is done. What I’m less sure about is whether the tool existing means people have it. The more I look, the more those seem like two different things.

A month ago I joined one of the frontier AI labs building this. From there it’s easy to think the access problem is solved: the thing is on the phone people already own, it answers in their language, and the basic version costs nothing. But when I look at who actually uses it well, it’s mostly people who already had the earlier kinds of access. They read English, they have a laptop and an office job, they have spare time, and a colleague once sat with them for an afternoon and showed them. If the requirements are gone, why are the same people left out as before, for the same reasons?

Before 2022 I didn’t know how to code, and I learned one concept at a time with each new model that came out, because I already read English and I already liked this stuff. I think slowly and English isn’t my first language, and it still got to me. I don’t think it reaches most people that way. Somebody hands it to them, or nobody does.

I keep thinking about the people nobody hands it to. The grandmother whose phone already has it installed, who uses the phone for calls and photos, and nobody in the family has thought to show her it can read the letter from the hospital out loud and explain it in the words she uses. The sixteen-year-old whose school banned it, so the only thing he’s learned about it is that it’s cheating, while a school forty minutes away runs a course on it.

Or the man who cleans offices at night in a country whose language isn’t his, who walks past thirty screens with it left open and has never once been told it’s also for him. Or the woman who tried it once, typed her question the way she’d ask a person at a counter, got a strange answer, felt stupid, and never opened it again, because nobody told her the second question is the one that works.

**None of these people are less intelligent than the ones who use it every day, and if they already have a phone the price is close to zero. So what is missing?** I think it’s a person they trust who sits next to them, in their language, without making them feel slow, and says: try it on your problem, I’ll stay while you do. That’s what I mean when I say access is a distribution problem. It’s slow, it happens one person at a time, it doesn’t scale, and almost nobody is paid to do it. Companies ship features and governments write strategies, and I don’t see who, in between, is meant to do the showing.

For the people above, the hard part is asking. If you’ve been told all your life that you’re slow, or foreign, or not the type, you don’t walk up to someone and ask them to teach you the new thing. So if it’s going to happen at all, the person who already has it has to go first.

That’s the part I find uncomfortable, because it includes me, and probably most people reading this: we already use the tool without thinking about it. We didn’t only earn that. We were also early, and being early was partly luck. I don’t think luck is something to feel guilty about, but I do wonder what we owe for it, and the only answer I have is a small one: sit with someone we know, start from their problem instead of from what the tool is, don’t touch their keyboard, and go back a week later to ask what broke.

We still talk about AI as if the hard part were inventing it. That part is done. The part that isn’t done is handing it over, and no new model is going to do that for us.

Nadir
