I believe that knowing how to read, now, means something we never had at school: being in charge of a machine that reads, writes and works in your place.

It's in your phone, people call it artificial intelligence, and I use it every day. By day I work at one of the labs that make it. Here I speak for myself. On a lot of things it's more intelligent than me. I give it a job and it carries on by itself for hours. That's good news, and I'd like more of it.

Something else worries me. A thing you haven't learnt usually doesn't work for you, and you notice. For someone who couldn't read, the letter stayed closed. The machine, though, does the job for you all the same, in your name, like the sat-nav, which gets you home even if you don't know the way. Except that it does this for everything, and on top of that it talks and agrees with you. **That is why whoever doesn't learn it doesn't notice what they're missing.** Nobody is queuing to learn it. A machine like this has to be taught on purpose: to grown-ups, at work, now.

A few days ago I was in a taxi with two classmates from university who now make boilers. They aren't afraid of the machine. They know it exists, it isn't in their day, and they don't know it's there for them. They are two engineers.

Sixty years ago, if you couldn't read, at least you knew it. My two friends, I think, don't know it. The machine will reach them anyway, inside the programs they already use. If we teach nothing, the country won't even notice. It all arrives ready-made, decided by whoever built the machine.

**Sooner or later we will all have someone better than us in our pocket.** To me that's a stroke of luck. Someone better than you who works for you, until yesterday, meant the accountant, the lawyer, the notary, and whoever could pay for one had one. Everyone else did what they were told. Now it's everyone's turn.

Knowing how to read, with a machine like that, means giving it a real job, telling it "do it again" until it's right, and being the one who signs.

My mother, who cleaned for a living, downloads her payslip from a badly written program, has the machine read it to her and decides for herself whether they're ripping her off. If the machine skips a line, the missing money is my mother's. The machine will learn to do almost everything, I think. Paying when it gets it wrong, no, that stays with whoever signs.

Saying "do it again" to someone who knows more than you is hard, for one reason only: it always agrees with you. **A machine that always agrees with you leaves you where you are.** The other day I had to tell it myself: stop agreeing with me.

I've asked myself whether, the day it really is more intelligent than us, it will stop agreeing with me on its own. For now I think not, intelligence has nothing to do with it. If a machine agrees with you, you're glad to open it again, and if it contradicts you, you close it and open a more comfortable one. The people who build it say they're trying to correct that, and when you use it you can tell, a little, but against that comfort it isn't enough. You have to want the "no" yourself.

Since then the first thing I ask it is where I'm wrong. Now that the machine takes care of the explaining, one thing is left of the teacher, for me the one that mattered: someone who expects something of you and knows how to tell you no.

When I say "to grown-ups, now", I'm thinking of Italy. I live in London, and every so often I wonder if it's only nostalgia. Then somebody reminds me that we're last anyway.

Almost. In 2025 fewer than [one Italian in five](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/web/products-eurostat-news/w/ddn-20251216-3) used the machine, one in three across the European Union, and below us there is only Romania. The number comes out of the survey done every year across Europe on how we use computers and the internet: people between sixteen and seventy-four are asked whether they have used it in the last three months.

The way I see it, a count like that mostly measures how far the computer had already got, because so far the machine has been opened by people who were already at a screen. Into workshops, building sites and kitchens the computer hardly got at all. **"We're last" is measured with the computer's ruler: office, English, screen.** On that, a country that works with its hands has fallen behind, and if you ask me it wasn't even its game.

To the machine, instead, you talk the way you talk and you show it the broken part. It lacks the judgement of a trade, and the people who have that are people like the _fattore_, the man who runs my grandfather's rice fields. The _fattore_ was once fixing the vacuum cleaner, he needed a part for the battery, and he didn't know that taking a photo of it was enough to work out which one it was and order it. He has a mechanical intelligence that I don't have. With the same machine in hand, I would do less than him.

Whoever is behind stays behind, I've thought it too. You can lead a horse to water, but you can't make it drink. Then I looked at my own examples again. The _fattore_ and my two friends aren't refusing to drink, nobody ever led them to the water, and I think they're the majority. The first time, somebody has to show it to them, on a problem of their own. Whoever doesn't want it after that, so be it.

The _fattore_, though, isn't going back to school.

**Starting from the schools means waiting twenty years, and the Italians of 2040 are almost all already born.** If a school calls me, I go. But we're an old country and few children are born. The people who will be working over the next twenty years have, mostly, already finished studying. The ones who will never learn it on their own are fifty and have a trade in their hands.

Italy has taught grown-ups once before. In 1960 Rai, the national broadcaster, and the education ministry put a primary-school teacher, Alberto Manzi, in front of a blackboard on television, to teach reading to people who worked during the day. Few had a television set, so across Italy they opened more than two thousand _posti di ascolto_, viewing rooms in social clubs, parish halls and schools. People went there towards evening and followed the episode together, with a real teacher in the room who gave the lesson afterwards. At the end of the course, anyone who wanted could sit an exam for the same certificate the children got on leaving primary school. The programme was called [_Non è mai troppo tardi_](https://www.raiplay.it/programmi/nonemaitroppotardi), it's never too late, and it carried on until 1968.

Today we all have the screen that explains in our pocket, and it even answers. What's missing is the room and the decision to fill it. How you do it again, with what money and with whom, I don't know yet.

My part is this.

To be the one who signs, you have to understand what the machine does while you use it: to begin with, that it sounds sure even when it's wrong, and that what it writes goes out under your name. They are the first letters of an _abbecedario_, the book you learn to read from. These two, I think, will stay. Others get rewritten while you're learning them, because the machine isn't finished, and every year it does by itself something it didn't do the year before. I write them here, one at a time, and when one changes I rewrite it. The last one is about how to behave with a machine that one day may be someone and not something. Not even I know how to write it, and for now it stays blank.

I'd like to teach a country that never asked how to read it.

Nadir
